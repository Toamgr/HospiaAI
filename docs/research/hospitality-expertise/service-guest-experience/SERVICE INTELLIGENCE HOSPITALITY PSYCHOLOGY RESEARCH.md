# HESTIA Service Intelligence Framework

HESTIA should model hospitality as a **relational operating system**, not a script engine. The system it needs to understand is not only whether a room was clean, a cocktail was correct, or a table was turned on time. It must understand whether the guest felt **safe, recognized, well-guided, unpressured, competently cared for, and worth remembering**. Research across social psychology, service design, hospitality management, and leading operator cultures converges on the same point: guests judge service through fast interpersonal inferences, nonverbal cues, service “clues” in the environment, fairness in recovery, and the emotional meaning of how execution is delivered. World-class operators formalize those judgments as culture, not as robotic scripts. 

Great hospitality drives trust, comfort, memory, emotion, and loyalty because it reduces uncertainty, signals good intent, communicates competence, protects dignity, and creates moments guests can later remember as personally meaningful. Hotel and restaurant research links experience and delight to loyalty; restaurant and hotel studies also show that nonverbal communication, memorable experiences, and place attachment materially shape satisfaction and repeat intention. 

## First principles of hospitality understanding

**Service versus hospitality.** Danny Meyer’s distinction remains the clearest operating definition: **service** is the technical delivery of a product; **hospitality** is how that delivery makes the recipient feel. He goes further: service is a monologue, while hospitality is a dialogue rooted in listening and responding from the guest’s side. Four Seasons expresses the same idea institutionally when it defines itself through “a deeply instilled ethic of personal service,” and Ritz-Carlton does it operationally by training employees to respond to both expressed and unexpressed wishes and needs. In other words, service is task accuracy; hospitality is relational interpretation of that accuracy. 

**Hosted versus processed.** A guest feels hosted when the venue’s system bends toward their purpose, pace, and dignity rather than forcing them to adapt to the venue’s convenience. Danny Meyer defines hospitality as the feeling that “the other person is on your side,” and Forbes Travel Guide’s guest-experience criteria explicitly test whether the stay felt “naturally personalized” and whether the guest did **not** feel like “one of many.” Service design research reaches the same conclusion: guests infer care, importance, and trust from small details and from how the environment and people communicate those relational messages. A guest feels processed when the system is legible only to staff, when the guest must repeatedly restate context, when waiting is exposed or awkward, or when attention feels standardized rather than situational. 

**What great hospitality professionals read.** The best operators are reading not only explicit requests but also emotional state: uncertainty, urgency, celebration, desire for privacy, appetite for conversation, need for control, irritation, embarrassment, overwhelm, and openness to guidance. Research on thin slices shows that people make meaningful interpersonal judgments from very brief observations, and hospitality research shows that employee nonverbal communication shapes guest emotions and satisfaction. Meyer’s own view is even sharper: for some guests, hospitality means a warmer “hug”; for others, it means “leave them the hell alone.” So the real signal is not “be friendlier”; it is “detect the guest’s preferred relational distance.” 

**Trust in the first 30 seconds.** Trust begins with the two universal dimensions of first impression: **warmth** and **competence**. Social-perception research shows these dimensions organize how people judge others; face and voice studies show trait inferences form in as little as 100 milliseconds from faces and about 500 milliseconds from voices. In hospitality terms, the first 30 seconds are an integrated bundle of cues: greeting quality, posture, eye contact, vocal steadiness, visible command of the situation, environmental order, and whether the guest appears oriented rather than abandoned. A clean, coherent setting influences expectations before the service itself is consumed, and service-evidence research shows guests interpret even nonverbal and environmental details as signals of care, importance, and trust. 

**What creates discomfort, pressure, or distance.** Discomfort comes from cue conflict: friendliness without competence, competence without warmth, attention without permission, speed without orientation, or personalization that feels invasive rather than considerate. Waiting without acknowledgement, being over-interrupted, visible confusion among staff, public correction, abrupt language, cold procedural language, insincere smiling, and spatial or tonal miscalibration all push the encounter toward pressure or distance. Research on nonverbal communication shows kinesics, proxemics, and paralanguage influence positive and negative emotions; research on authentic displays shows positive affect works best when it feels sincere rather than performed. 

**How guests evaluate quality unconsciously.** Guests do not consciously score only the technical outcome. They infer quality from what Berry, Wall, and Carbone call **functional**, **mechanic**, and **humanic** clues: what was done, what surrounded it, and how people behaved while doing it. Bitner’s servicescape model makes the same point from the environmental side. Emotional-contagion research adds that displayed positive emotion can transfer to customers and influence quality evaluations, while service-encounter research shows displayed emotion shapes customers’ judgments of both the social interaction and the service delivery. HESTIA therefore cannot reduce quality to timing, accuracy, and check averages; it must model the clue system guests actually use. 

**Tone, timing, body language, pacing, and language.** Tone should signal calm certainty and beneficial intent. Timing should respect the guest’s purpose, not the server’s rhythm. Body language should be open, composed, and attentive without crowding. Pacing should be modulated to occasion: business diners often want fewer interruptions; celebrants may welcome more ceremonial pacing; some bar guests want interaction while others want quick invisibility. Language should be concrete enough to reassure guests that staff understood the request, since research shows linguistic concreteness improves customer satisfaction. Smiling helps, but eye gaze and authenticity matter; smiling alone is not enough. 

**Warm without informal.** Warmth does not require slang, oversharing, or collapsing professional distance. It is conveyed by focused listening, respectful address, anticipatory help, calm eye contact, specific follow-through, and emotional congruence. Les Clefs d’Or standards explicitly pair attentiveness with professional grammar, professional address, and confidentiality. Warmth in hospitality is therefore not “casualness”; it is **respect plus attunement**. 

**Premium without cold.** Luxury-hospitality research indicates that both warmth and competence matter more in luxury settings than in affordable ones, but competence has greater primacy in luxury hotels, whereas warmth has greater primacy in affordable hotels. That means premium service should feel composed, precise, discreet, and anticipatory, yet still human. Four Seasons codifies this as empathy through the Golden Rule, and Ritz-Carlton codifies it as personal, memorable experiences plus immediate problem ownership and privacy protection. Premium becomes cold when expertise is signaled without side-taking; warmth becomes low-end when it abandons precision and discretion. 

**How great teams recover from failure.** Strong recovery combines justice, speed, ownership, empathy, and follow-through. Ritz-Carlton explicitly teaches employees to own and immediately resolve guest problems. Danny Meyer operationalizes recovery through awareness, acknowledgement, apology, action, and additional generosity. Service-recovery research consistently shows that distributive, procedural, and interactional justice matter; that speed and apology affect post-recovery satisfaction; and that emotional recovery can be more effective than pure economic recovery at eliciting forgiveness because it activates empathy. Severity matters: the more severe the failure, the more revenge risk rises and the less easy forgiveness becomes. Recovery is therefore not just compensation; it is emotional repair plus credible control restoration. 

## Complete Service Intelligence Framework

The framework below translates hospitality research into the categories HESTIA should operationalize. The discipline behind all of them is the same: **store service-relevant facts and outcomes, not speculative psychological diagnoses or intrusive personal profiling**. That principle is consistent with concierge confidentiality norms and with newer research showing that AI personalization in hospitality becomes less effective when privacy concerns rise. 

**Guest Emotional Signals.** **Why it matters:** emotion shapes satisfaction, forgiveness, memory, and loyalty. **Signals to capture:** uncertainty, urgency, celebration, frustration, fatigue, social openness, privacy-seeking, embarrassment, and desire for guidance. **How humans notice it:** gaze patterns, question density, response latency, brevity, voice tension, body closure, glancing at exits/companions/clock, pace of speech, and whether the guest is leaning in or away. **How AI could support:** prompt staff when emotional state appears to be shifting, flag mismatch between staff energy and guest mood, and surface prior successful approaches. **What data should be stored:** service-relevant tags such as “prefers low interruption,” “celebration kept private,” or “wants more explanation.” **What should not be stored:** mental-health speculation, grief labels, inferred relationship problems, or raw biometric emotion judgments. **How often it changes:** minute to minute. **How it should affect future recommendations:** mostly through proximity, pacing, tone, and intervention timing, not through permanent personality labels. 

**First Impression Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** early warmth-and-competence judgments anchor the rest of the encounter. **Signals to capture:** greeting latency, acknowledgement, orientation clarity, staff posture, eye contact, vocal steadiness, visible confusion, crowding, environmental order, and whether the guest had to ask “what happens next.” **How humans notice it:** the room feels either assured or shaky almost immediately. **How AI could support:** alert teams to unacknowledged arrivals, cluttered entrances, queue exposure, or slow greeting at key touchpoints. **What data should be stored:** arrival time, greeting time, handoff ownership, queue friction, entrance condition. **What should not be stored:** face recognition profiles without explicit permission or covert identity matching. **How often it changes:** every arrival. **How it should affect future recommendations:** if first impressions repeatedly fail, HESTIA should prioritize staffing, entry choreography, signage, and host coaching before upsell or retention tactics. 

**Tone and Language Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** wording changes whether guests feel understood, reassured, or managed. **Signals to capture:** concreteness versus vagueness, positive framing, apology quality, professional address, interruption phrases, and whether staff are speaking in brand-appropriate rather than internal or defensive language. **How humans notice it:** guests relax when language is specific and respectful; they tense when it sounds canned, abstract, dismissive, or bureaucratic. **How AI could support:** suggest clearer, more concrete phrasing; flag habitual negative formulations; surface occasion-sensitive phrasing; preserve brand formality while avoiding stiffness. **What data should be stored:** successful phrasing patterns by venue type, complaint-response outcomes, preferred forms of address if voluntarily shared. **What should not be stored:** fully transcribed personal conversations unless lawfully consented and clearly necessary. **How often it changes:** by venue, staff member, and situation. **How it should affect future recommendations:** refine brand voice, recovery language, confirmation messaging, and manager coaching. 

**Timing and Pacing Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** poor pacing makes even technically excellent service feel intrusive or neglectful. **Signals to capture:** wait exposure, order-to-delivery rhythm, interruption cadence, check timing, recovery speed, and guest pace preference. **How humans notice it:** guests either feel “taken care of in rhythm” or “rushed/ignored.” **How AI could support:** model pace by occasion, table, bar seat, room journey, and event phase; recommend when to check in and when not to. **What data should be stored:** preferred pace, prior successful cadence, service timestamps, delay points. **What should not be stored:** speculative reasons for slowness attributed to individual staff without evidence. **How often it changes:** by occasion and by service period. **How it should affect future recommendations:** seating, server loads, host estimates, course pacing, and interruption rules for business or intimate occasions. 

**Body Language Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** guests infer friendliness, confidence, and trust from posture, gaze, movement, distance, and vocal delivery. **Signals to capture:** open/closed stance, direct eye gaze, smile authenticity, proxemics, rushed movement, looming, shoulder tension, and vocal harshness or softness. **How humans notice it:** “they looked pleased to help” versus “they were somewhere else.” **How AI could support:** coach on high-level patterns such as crowding, avoidance, or over-hovering; detect floor areas or service phases where negative nonverbal patterns spike. **What data should be stored:** pattern summaries and training observations. **What should not be stored:** persistent biometric profiles, facial-emotion archives, or punitive micro-scoring on isolated moments. **How often it changes:** continuously, with fatigue and rush levels. **How it should affect future recommendations:** staff breaks, station rotation, service choreography, and coaching on entry, approach, and exit. 

**Service Recovery Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** mistakes do not destroy loyalty as predictably as badly handled mistakes do. **Signals to capture:** failure type, severity, ownership speed, apology presence, remedy offered, guest emotional trajectory, and whether the issue was closed credibly. **How humans notice it:** not merely whether compensation appeared, but whether someone took the guest’s side and restored control. **How AI could support:** triage severity, recommend recovery pathways, prompt visible ownership, and ensure closure is documented. **What data should be stored:** factual timeline, remedy, responsible owner, guest response, follow-up result. **What should not be stored:** blame-heavy narratives or gossip. **How often it changes:** episodically but with lasting consequences. **How it should affect future recommendations:** future service should acknowledge the recovery history carefully, avoid repeat triggers, and sometimes route the guest to stronger operators. 

**Complaint Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** complaints are prediction systems for churn, revenge behavior, reputational damage, and training gaps. **Signals to capture:** complaint tone, channel, recurrence, staff response style, justice perception, and whether the guest sought punishment or resolution. **How humans notice it:** some complaints ask to be heard; others are already escalations. **How AI could support:** separate constructive complaints from high-risk public complaints, recommend accommodative rather than defensive posture, surface repeat root causes, and track unresolved loops. **What data should be stored:** issue taxonomy, severity, root cause, public/private channel, response effectiveness. **What should not be stored:** character judgments about “difficult guests” as a durable identity. **How often it changes:** incident-based, but patterns may persist. **How it should affect future recommendations:** staffing, training, menu/service redesign, and selective outreach. 

**VIP Service Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** VIP service is less about flattery than about privacy, seamlessness, and lower exposure to friction. **Signals to capture:** confidentiality requirements, exposure sensitivity, service ownership, timing sensitivity, preferred access points, and do-not-disturb parameters. **How humans notice it:** elite service feels invisible, not theatrical. **How AI could support:** suppress unnecessary alerts, route to trusted operators, reduce repeated asks, and ensure cross-department continuity. **What data should be stored:** explicit service preferences, privacy instructions, named owner, approved logistical notes. **What should not be stored:** celebrity speculation, personal gossip, or unnecessary family/health details. **How often it changes:** slowly, unless circumstances change. **How it should affect future recommendations:** service should become more discreet, anticipatory, and continuity-driven, not more familiar or intrusive. 

**Regular Guest Intelligence.** **Why it matters:** recognition reduces friction and builds the feeling of being known, but over-recognition can feel creepy. **Signals to capture:** favorite table, server, wine style, pace, room preferences, celebration patterns, aversions, and past recoveries. **How humans notice it:** strong hosts remember useful details and avoid making the guest repeat themselves. **How AI could support:** surface only the few highest-value preferences at the right moment and retire stale ones. **What data should be stored:** permissioned preference memory with timestamps and confidence labels. **What should not be stored:** speculative social-media profiling beyond clear service relevance and guest comfort. **How often it changes:** slowly, but not never. **How it should affect future recommendations:** create continuity first, delight second; reuse what worked, but leave room for freshness. 

**Staff Performance Signals.** **Why it matters:** hospitality quality depends on emotional performance as well as technical execution, and emotional labor has real costs. **Signals to capture:** composure under pressure, recovery ownership, handoff quality, consistency of nonverbal signals, guest-specific adaptation, and fatigue indicators. **How humans notice it:** some staff create calm and trust; others technically perform but spread tension. **How AI could support:** distinguish technical misses from relational misses, detect burnout patterns, and coach for adaptability rather than fake positivity. **What data should be stored:** aggregated performance trends, coaching history, recovery outcomes, peer/manager observations. **What should not be stored:** invasive emotional-surveillance archives or simplistic “smile scores.” **How often it changes:** daily and by shift load. **How it should affect future recommendations:** inform lineup topics, pairings, rotation, and individualized coaching. 

**Manager Observation Framework.** **Why it matters:** managers are the primary real-time interpreters of service drift. **Signals to capture:** entrance control, host clarity, table/bar coverage, visible waiting, handoff failures, complaint ownership, team tension, and whether the room feels coherent. **How humans notice it:** experienced leaders scan for “energy breakdowns” before metrics move. **How AI could support:** convert observations into a shared language, highlight blind spots, and compare what managers noticed with what guests later reported. **What data should be stored:** structured shift observations tied to zones, times, and outcomes. **What should not be stored:** free-form character attacks. **How often it changes:** every shift. **How it should affect future recommendations:** drive next-shift lineup priorities, staffing changes, and manager development. 

**Service Standards Memory.** **Why it matters:** culture breaks when standards are implicit, inconsistent, or forgotten under pressure. **Signals to capture:** venue promise, non-negotiables, sequence expectations, sensory standards, privacy rules, and recovery rules. **How humans notice it:** great venues feel coherent because the same values reappear across touchpoints. **How AI could support:** maintain a current, venue-specific memory of standards and compare live observations against it. **What data should be stored:** standards by venue type, occasion adaptations, “always/never” rules, gold-standard examples. **What should not be stored:** obsolete standards that were never formally retired. **How often it changes:** slowly, with periodic revision. **How it should affect future recommendations:** use standards as the baseline for coaching, audits, and anomaly detection. 

**Hospitality Culture.** **Why it matters:** hospitality is reproduced culturally before it is reproduced procedurally. Danny Meyer’s “enlightened hospitality,” Four Seasons’ dignity-and-respect philosophy, Ritz-Carlton’s employee promise, and Disney’s hiring-training-care-communication model all show that internal treatment conditions external warmth. **Signals to capture:** whether staff feel supported, whether standards are discussed daily, whether team members help across silos, and whether recognition stories circulate. **How humans notice it:** guests feel culture through consistency, morale, and recovery confidence. **How AI could support:** identify where culture is being translated into behavior and where it is not. **What data should be stored:** story library, lineup themes, recognition events, team climate trends. **What should not be stored:** faux-culture slogans detached from behavior. **How often it changes:** slowly but cumulatively. **How it should affect future recommendations:** prioritize culture repairs before adding more guest-facing automation. 

**Training Recommendations.** **Why it matters:** hospitality skill must be coached in context, not only taught abstractly. **Signals to capture:** repeated misses in entry, pacing, apology, guest reading, language clarity, and cross-department continuity. **How humans notice it:** trainers hear the same recovery failures or awkward openings repeating across shifts. **How AI could support:** convert pattern detection into short, venue-specific drills and pre-shift coaching themes; recommend practice around real moments, not generic modules. **What data should be stored:** training history, observed improvement, example moments, coachability indicators. **What should not be stored:** humiliating highlight reels or punitive ranking by emotional style. **How often it changes:** weekly to monthly. **How it should affect future recommendations:** drive micro-learning, role-play priorities, shadowing assignments, and manager follow-up. 

**Service Risk Signals.** **Why it matters:** risk often appears before the formal complaint—through subtle pressure, privacy concern, cultural mismatch, burnout, or over-personalization. **Signals to capture:** repeated queue discomfort, high staff strain, defensive complaint answers, privacy-sensitive guests receiving overly personal references, service coldness in premium contexts, and severe-failure clusters. **How humans notice it:** the room is technically functioning but emotionally fraying. **How AI could support:** warn managers when privacy, fairness, tone, or fatigue risks are rising; suppress “clever personalization” when it may feel invasive. **What data should be stored:** aggregated risk patterns and confirmed incidents. **What should not be stored:** hidden surveillance artifacts or speculative “high-maintenance guest” labels. **How often it changes:** daily, and sharply during peaks. **How it should affect future recommendations:** trigger de-escalation, staffing changes, privacy guardrails, and review of automation boundaries. 

## What HESTIA should understand, observe, remember, coach, warn about, and never automate

HESTIA’s true Service Intelligence Layer should understand five interacting models at once: the **guest state model** (emotion, purpose, privacy, pace), the **service contract model** (what this venue promises), the **interaction model** (warmth, competence, timing, body language, handoffs), the **recovery model** (severity, justice, closure), and the **culture model** (how this team reliably produces hospitality under load). If it understands only CRM facts or POS events, it will optimize operations while missing hospitality. 

HESTIA should **observe** visible friction and emotional drift before they become failures: greeting delays, unclear waits, interruption overload, visible confusion, repeat questions, handoff breaks, unresolved complaints, over-familiar language, defensive tone, cold-premium drift, and fatigue-linked nonverbal deterioration. It should prioritize “small clues” because research shows those clues disproportionately shape guest judgment. 

HESTIA should **remember** only service-relevant, permissioned memory: preferences, cadence, aversions, favorite tables, preferred servers, prior recoveries, privacy instructions, and what kind of gestures worked or failed. It should store the **minimum durable memory** needed to make the next interaction feel smoother and more humane. Meyer’s examples are instructive here: favorite table, wine category, server, pace, business-dinner interruption level, celebration context. 

HESTIA should **coach** staff and managers primarily on principles and patterns: how to open with warmth and certainty, how to adjust pacing by occasion, how to recover with ownership and justice, how to be warm without becoming informal, and how to be premium without becoming cold. Training should be rooted in real moments from that venue’s own service history and delivered as pre-shift micro-coaching, not as decontextualized manuals. 

HESTIA should **warn about** three classes of damage. The first is **relational damage**: guests feeling unseen, rushed, exposed, or talked at. The second is **cultural damage**: staff burnout, defensive complaint handling, and brittle standards that collapse under pressure. The third is **trust damage**: creepy personalization, privacy breaches, excessive automation, or substitutions of AI fluency for human accountability. New hospitality AI research suggests that perceived warmth and competence matter for AI acceptance too, but privacy concerns and authenticity boundaries remain decisive. 

HESTIA should aim to **improve** six outcomes over time: faster trust formation, lower visible friction, better pace fit, better recovery closure, stronger regular-guest continuity, and healthier staff/service culture. Four Seasons, Ritz-Carlton, Disney, and Forbes Travel Guide all implicitly treat consistency plus humanity as the path to this improvement; the difference for HESTIA is that it can make those patterns measurable without reducing them to a script score. 

HESTIA should **never automate** the parts of hospitality where authenticity, discretion, and moral ownership are the product. That includes the real apology, the judgment call in a sensitive complaint, the decision about whether to make a personal gesture, the interpretation of grief or intimacy, the handling of VIP confidentiality, and the conversion of a damaged moment into a “great last chapter.” AI may recommend, remind, or route, but the human host, manager, maître d’, bartender, concierge, or GM must remain the accountable author of those moments. This is supported both by hospitality culture models and by recent research showing that human warmth and authenticity remain central in emotionally meaningful service contexts. 

## Ideal Service Intelligence Loop

The ideal loop for HESTIA is a **closed learning system** that converts live service into better next service without turning people into objects of surveillance. The loop should be designed around moments, context, and culture rather than around transactions alone. 

| Loop stage | What happens | What HESTIA should ingest | What HESTIA should output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-shift service briefing | Team aligns on arrivals, occasions, risks, standards, and staffing realities | Reservations, house count, event flow, VIP/privacy flags, unresolved issues, prior shift notes, complaint carryovers | A short briefing: who needs discretion, who needs pacing changes, where risk is highest, which standard to reinforce |
| Live service observation inputs | Managers and leads record visible friction and exceptional moments in real time | Greeting delays, queue discomfort, pace misses, tone or body-language drift, handoff failures, recovery starts | Smart prompts: who needs help now, which tables/zones are drifting, where standards are breaking |
| Staff notes | Frontline teams add useful service memory after meaningful interactions | Preference confirmations, pace notes, aversions, successful gestures, guest feedback, recovery details | Cleaned memory objects with confidence and expiry rules |
| Guest feedback | Direct feedback and public feedback are consolidated | Surveys, reviews, thank-you notes, complaint text, ratings | Pattern summaries by venue, daypart, staff behavior, and occasion |
| Complaint recovery | Every failure enters a structured justice-and-closure workflow | Failure type, severity, apology, action, compensation, owner, closure status | Recovery recommendation, escalation routing, follow-up reminders, repeat-trigger blocking |
| Manager review | Shift leaders review room performance and team signals against standards | Observation notes, service timestamps, guest sentiment, recovery outcomes | Root-cause view: process problem, training problem, staffing problem, or culture problem |
| AI pattern detection | The system learns across shifts and venues | All structured service objects, weighted by recency and confidence | Recurring patterns: late greetings, cold premium drift, over-interruption, specific stations with complaint spikes |
| Training recommendation | Patterns are translated into short operational coaching | Observation trends, feedback clusters, recovery misses | Micro-drills, shadowing assignments, lineup topics, manager coaching notes |
| Next-shift coaching | Learning returns to the floor immediately | Prior shift lessons and unresolved priorities | Targeted pre-shift coaching, not generic training |
| Long-term service culture memory | The venue builds self-knowledge over months and years | Gold-standard stories, standards revisions, recurring failure patterns, successful recovery playbooks | A growing “service playbook” that reflects the venue’s real hospitality DNA rather than an imported template |

In practice, the loop should work like this. Before service, HESTIA briefs the team on who is arriving, what emotional contracts are likely in play, where risk is concentrated, and what recoveries remain open. During service, it accepts lightweight live observations and highlights only the most important deviations. After service, it turns staff notes and guest feedback into durable but privacy-safe memory. Then it compares those memories against standards and recurring outcomes, converts them into manager reviews and training priorities, and feeds them back into the next shift. Over time, this produces the one thing most hospitality software does not: **long-term service culture memory**. 

The strongest design principle for the loop is that **every stored fact should justify a better future act of hospitality**. If a datum will not help a team welcome better, pace better, recover better, protect privacy better, or coach better, HESTIA should not keep it. That principle preserves trust with guests and staff while keeping the system faithful to the logic of hospitality rather than drifting into surveillance or generic CRM accumulation. 

## Open questions and limits

Some parts of this framework are direct findings from research; others are system-design inferences for HESTIA. The evidence base is strongest on first impressions, warmth/competence, servicescapes, nonverbal communication, emotional contagion, service recovery justice, luxury-service culture, and personalization/privacy tradeoffs. It is weaker on publicly documented fine-dining maître d’ operating methods and on uniform, open-access standards from brands like Disney or Four Seasons beyond their published philosophies. For those areas, the framework above intentionally avoids false precision and treats venue-specific implementation as something HESTIA should learn locally over time rather than assume universally. 
